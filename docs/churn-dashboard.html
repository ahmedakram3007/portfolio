What actually drives churn

Churn rate broken out by five dimensions — SQL queries for each in sql/analysis_queries.sql.

By contract type

The single strongest driver in this dataset.

By tenure

New customers are by far the highest risk.

By internet service

Fiber customers churn over 5x more than customers with no internet service.

By payment method

Manual payment methods correlate strongly with churn.

By add-on services (Online Security + Tech Support)

Customers with both add-ons churn at roughly a fifth the rate of customers with neither — the clearest actionable lever in the data.
03

What doesn't predict churn — a genuinely useful negative result

Customer satisfaction score (1–5) and support interaction sentiment both show almost no correlation with churn here — churn rate stays flat at roughly 25–28% regardless of satisfaction score, and regardless of whether a customer's support interactions were positive, neutral, or negative. That's a slightly counter-intuitive finding worth taking seriously: it suggests this business's churn problem is structural — contract terms, price, and tenure — rather than a service-quality problem. A retention strategy built around "improve support quality" would likely miss the mark; one built around contract incentives and onboarding would not.

Predictive model

Two classifiers on an 80/20 train/test split, evaluated against a 73.4% baseline (always predicting "no churn").

ModelAccuracyPrecisionRecallF1ROC-AUC
Logistic Regression 0.804 0.648 0.575 0.609 0.836
Random Forest (balanced) 0.739 0.506 0.802 0.621 0.838

For a retention use case, recall usually matters more than precision — a missed churner is a lost customer, a false positive just means an unnecessary retention offer. The class-balanced Random Forest trades precision for recall accordingly.

Top model features

Random Forest feature importances — confirms the SQL findings above: tenure, contract length, and charges dominate.
